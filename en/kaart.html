Roots

  • West African music traditions1700 · West and Central Africa
    Between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries more than twelve million Africans were shipped to the Americas as slaves, most of them from West and Central Africa.
  • British and Irish folk music1700 · Scotland, Ireland and England
    From the eighteenth century onwards, immigrants from Scotland, England and Ireland settled in the Appalachians.
  • European art music1750 · Europe
    From Europe came the harmony of art music: major and minor, chords with a fixed function, melodies in regular phrases, and music that was written down.
  • Work songs and field hollers1800 · Plantations in the southern US
    On the cotton and sugar plantations of the southern United States, enslaved Africans sang while they worked.
  • March music and brass bands1880 · Washington
    By the end of the nineteenth century almost every American town had a brass band, and John Philip Sousa, leader of the United States Marine Band in Washington, wrote marches such as The Stars and Stripes Forever (1896).
  • Spirituals and gospel1890 · Southern US
    Spirituals emerged in the nineteenth century among enslaved African Americans in the southern United States; in the Black churches of the 1920s and 1930s they grew into gospel: religious songs in several voices, with call and response.
  • Delta blues1890 · Mississippi Delta
    The blues grew up around 1890 out of the work songs, hollers and spirituals of the Black rural population of the South, with the Mississippi Delta as a key region.
  • Ragtime1895 · Sedalia (Missouri)
    Ragtime was the first African American musical style to be sold on a large scale as sheet music.
  • Appalachian folk (mountain music)1900 · Appalachia
    From the eighteenth century onwards, immigrants from Scotland, England and Ireland brought ballads and dance music with them to the Appalachians.
  • Classic blues1920 · New York
    In the 1920s the best-selling blues records were made by women.
  • Boogie-woogie1928 · Chicago
    Boogie-woogie is a piano style that grew out of the blues, played in bars and at house parties in the South and later in Chicago.
  • Chicago blues1948 · Chicago
    After the Second World War many Black Americans moved from the South to Chicago, and the blues went with them.
  • Celtic folk1962 · Dublin
    Celtic folk is Irish traditional music played as a band.

Jazz

  • New Orleans jazz1900 · New Orleans
    In New Orleans, around 1900, jazz grew out of the brass bands; later it was also called Dixieland or hot jazz.
  • Swing and big band1930 · New York
    In the 1930s the New Orleans band grew into the big band of twelve to seventeen musicians, with an arranger who wrote riffs for the sections.
  • Bebop1940 · Harlem, New York
    In the early forties young jazz musicians held jam sessions at Minton's Playhouse in Harlem after their work in the big bands.
  • Latin jazz1947 · New York
    From 1943 Machito and Mario Bauzá mixed jazz with Cuban music in New York.
  • Cool Jazz and modal jazz1949 · New York
    In 1949 and 1950 Miles Davis recorded with a nonet the sessions that were later called Birth of the Cool, with French horn and tuba alongside the usual horns and arrangements by Gil Evans, among others.
  • West Coast jazz1952 · Los Angeles
    In the fifties Cool Jazz developed its own variant in California, played by musicians who also worked in the film and television studios.
  • Hard bop1954 · New York
    On the East Coast many Black musicians found Cool Jazz too restrained.
  • Bossa nova1958 · Rio de Janeiro
    In Rio de Janeiro, guitarist and singer João Gilberto and composer Antônio Carlos Jobim created bossa nova from 1958: a soft, restrained version of samba, with jazz chords and a voice that almost whispers.
  • Free jazz1959 · New York
    Free jazz let go of the agreements about theme, chord progression, time signature and tempo: musicians often improvise at the same time and dissonance becomes raw material.
  • Fusion and jazz rock1969 · New York
    Miles Davis listened to Jimi Hendrix, Sly and the Family Stone and James Brown, and played electric instruments on In a Silent Way (1969) and, on Bitches Brew (1970), several drummers at once as well.
  • Smooth jazz1975 · United States
    In the mid-seventies a softer variant of fusion emerged, which became hugely popular in the eighties.
  • Nu jazz1990 · London
    In London from the late eighties DJs such as Gilles Peterson played acid jazz: jazz, funk and soul for the dance floor.

Country & folk

  • Country1927 · Nashville
    In the 1920s radio and the gramophone record turned mountain music into country.
  • Honky-tonk1940 · Beaumont
    Honky-tonk is country for the bar room.
  • Bluegrass1945 · Kentucky and Nashville
    From the forties a fast, acoustic variant emerged within country: bluegrass, named after the band of mandolin player Bill Monroe from Kentucky, the Blue Grass Boys.
  • Folk revival1955 · Greenwich Village, New York
    In the 1950s and 1960s young white musicians in Greenwich Village, New York, rediscovered the old folk music: the folk revival.
  • Nashville sound1957 · Nashville
    In the late fifties, country was losing its audience to rock 'n' roll.
  • Folk rock1965 · Los Angeles
    In folk rock, folk melodies are played on electric guitars.
  • Country rock1968 · Los Angeles
    At the end of the 1960s some rock musicians went back to the roots of American music; in 1968 The Byrds, with Gram Parsons, recorded Sweetheart of the Rodeo.
  • Outlaw country1972 · Austin
    In the early seventies, Waylon Jennings and Willie Nelson felt that the Nashville studios had too much say.
  • Indie folk2008 · Seattle
    Around 2008, young musicians brought back acoustic folk with the approach of indie rock: recording themselves and releasing on small record labels.

Rock

  • Rock 'n' roll1951 · Memphis
    Rock 'n' roll grew out of rhythm and blues, with influences from jump blues, boogie-woogie, gospel and country.
  • Rockabilly1954 · Memphis
    Rockabilly is the fast, stripped-down form of rock 'n' roll in which country and rhythm and blues come together.
  • Skiffle1956 · London
    In the mid-1950s skiffle swept through Britain as a craze, with roots in American folk and blues; Lonnie Donegan had a hit with Rock Island Line in 1956.
  • Indorock1957 · The Hague
    After 1949 Indo-Dutch people, who came to the Netherlands from the former Dutch East Indies, brought kroncong and the Hawaiian guitar with them; in the Netherlands this fused with American country and rock 'n' roll into indorock.
  • Surf rock1961 · Southern California
    In the early 1960s, young musicians in Southern California made surf rock: instrumental guitar music drenched in reverb, in which fast, repeated notes imitate the roar of the waves.
  • British beat and the British Invasion1962 · Liverpool
    After hundreds of hours in the clubs of Hamburg, The Beatles became the biggest band in Britain in 1963.
  • Nederbeat1964 · The Hague
    Around the time of the Beatles' visit in June 1964, beat bands sprang up all over the Netherlands, with The Hague as the centre: the Golden Earrings, Q65, the Motions and Shocking Blue.
  • Raga rock1965 · London
    In the mid-1960s Western bands began to use Indian instruments and scales: raga rock.
  • Garage rock1965 · United States
    After the British Invasion, teenagers all over America started bands, rehearsed in the garage and recorded raw singles with a few chords, a screaming singer and a cheap organ.
  • Blues rock1966 · London
    Cream, the trio of Eric Clapton, Jack Bruce and Ginger Baker, took the blues into the big halls, with long solos and loud amplifiers; Clapton played Robert Johnson's Cross Road Blues as Crossroads.
  • Psychedelic rock1966 · San Francisco
    Around 1966 and 1967 bands drew inspiration from Indian music, the classical avant-garde and LSD.
  • Art rock1967 · New York
    In New York, Lou Reed and John Cale's band The Velvet Underground worked with the artist Andy Warhol, who designed the cover with the banana.
  • Symphonic rock1967 · London
    Symphonic rock brought classical music into pop: orchestras, church organ, Mellotron and themes by Bach or Beethoven.
  • Hard rock and heavy metal1968 · London
    Hard rock grew out of the blues rock of Cream, Jimi Hendrix and The Yardbirds.
  • Progressive rock1969 · London
    Progressive rock turned the album into a composition, with long tracks, classical influences and complex time signatures.
  • Krautrock1970 · Düsseldorf
    In West Germany bands such as Can, Neu!
  • Southern rock1970 · Jacksonville (Florida)
    Southern rock emerged in the southern United States around 1970, with The Allman Brothers Band as its pioneers.
  • Soft rock and AOR1971 · Los Angeles
    From the late 1960s radio stations on the FM band played whole album sides: album-oriented rock.
  • Glam rock1972 · London
    Around 1972 rock developed a theatrical side: costumes, make-up and a stage persona became just as important as the music.
  • Powerpop1972 · Cleveland and Memphis
    Powerpop is guitar pop with the melodies and vocal harmonies of the early Beatles and the force of loud guitars.
  • Heartland rock1975 · New Jersey
    Bruce Springsteen from New Jersey made rock about ordinary people, work, cars and the dream of getting away.
  • Punk1975 · London
    Punk was the answer to rock that had become big, expensive and complicated: short, fast, loud songs with few chords, and the idea that anyone can start a band.
  • Boerenrock1975 · Achterhoek
    Normaal was founded in 1975 in the Achterhoek, a rural region in the east of the Netherlands, around singer Bennie Jolink, and sang in the local Achterhoek dialect.
  • Yacht rock1976 · Los Angeles
    Yacht rock is the smooth West Coast rock of the late seventies and early eighties.
  • New wave1977 · London
    New wave is an umbrella term for musicians who wanted to move on after punk, with more attention to atmosphere, melody and sound.
  • Postpunk1978 · Manchester
    Postpunk kept the energy and the do-it-yourself attitude of punk, but let go of the three chords.
  • Gothic rock1980 · Crawley
    Out of postpunk grew gothic rock: dark, echoing and slow.
  • NWOBHM and thrash metal1980 · London
    In the 1980s heavy metal became harder and faster.
  • Hardcore punk1980 · Los Angeles and Washington
    Around 1980, American bands made punk shorter, faster and louder: hardcore.
  • Glam metal and stadium rock1981 · Los Angeles
    After MTV launched in 1981, a rock band had to look good too.
  • Neo-prog1981 · Aylesbury
    Neo-prog emerged around 1981 in England, when progressive rock had gone out of fashion.
  • Alternative rock1983 · Athens (Georgia)
    Alongside the stadiums, a rock world of small labels, clubs and college radio grew up in the 1980s: alternative rock, known in America as college rock.
  • Rock in Dutch1984 · The Netherlands
    After Doe Maar, Dutch also became the language of rock.
  • Dream pop1984 · Grangemouth
    Dream pop sounds like a dream: soft, drenched in reverb, with vocals floating above the music.
  • Dialect pop and rock1985 · America (Limburg)
    From the 1980s onwards, more and more bands sang in their own regional language, not as a joke but to tell stories close to home about the village, the countryside and the family.
  • Emo1985 · Washington
    Emo began in the mid-1980s in Washington, when bands such as Rites of Spring played hardcore with personal, emotional lyrics: emotional hardcore.
  • Death metal1985 · Tampa
    Death metal emerged in the mid-eighties, when bands in Florida pushed the speed of hardcore punk and the darkness of heavy metal to the extreme.
  • Indie pop and shoegaze1986 · London
    From the late 1970s onwards, small British labels such as Rough Trade and Creation released guitar music outside the major record companies: indie, short for independent.
  • Power metal1987 · Hamburg
    Power metal is heavy metal that sounds fast, high and hopeful.
  • Funk rock1988 · New York
    Living Colour from New York mixed hard rock with funk on Cult of Personality (1988); guitarist Vernon Reid was part of the Black Rock Coalition.
  • Madchester1988 · Manchester
    Madchester was the dance scene of Manchester around 1989.
  • Grunge1989 · Seattle
    Around the small label Sub Pop in Seattle, a scene emerged in the late 1980s that mixed punk and heavy metal into a raw, heavy sound.
  • Riot grrrl1991 · Olympia (Washington)
    In the early 1990s, a feminist punk movement emerged in Olympia, in the state of Washington: riot grrrl.
  • Black metal1991 · Oslo
    The name comes from Venom, who released the album Black Metal in 1982.
  • Post-rock1991 · Glasgow
    Post-rock uses rock instruments, but not the form of the rock song.
  • Britpop1993 · London
    Around 1993, British bands deliberately took a different direction from American grunge: back to the Beatles, The Kinks, The Who and the mods.
  • Pop punk1994 · Berkeley (California)
    Pop punk combines the speed and guitars of punk with pop melodies and sing-along choruses.
  • Post-grunge1994 · Seattle
    Post-grunge is rock that takes over the heavy, raw guitar sound of grunge, but without the darkness, and with slickly produced choruses made for radio.
  • Nu metal1995 · Los Angeles
    Nu metal is metal combined with hip hop and electronics.
  • Symphonic metal1996 · The Netherlands
    Symphonic metal combines heavy guitars and drums with orchestral sounds, choirs and a classically trained voice.
  • Metalcore1998 · Boston
    Metalcore combines the riffs of heavy metal with the rage and the breakdowns of hardcore punk.
  • Garage rock revival2001 · New York (Lower East Side)
    In 2001 guitar rock suddenly sounded raw and short again.

Pop & songwriting

  • Fado1840 · Lisbon (Mouraria and Alfama)
    Fado emerged in the first half of the nineteenth century in the poor harbour districts of Lisbon, such as Mouraria and Alfama.
  • Minstrel shows1843 · New York
    From the 1830s onwards, white performers with blacked-up faces appeared in minstrel shows: blackface entertainments in which Black Americans were ridiculed, with songs, dances and sketches supposedly drawn from life on the plantation.
  • Tin Pan Alley1885 · New York
    From around 1885 music publishers set up shop close together in Manhattan, and from the 1890s mainly on West 28th Street.
  • Barbershop1895 · United States
    Barbershop is singing for four men without instruments: the lead sings the melody, the tenor sings above it, and the baritone and bass fill out the chord.
  • Rebetiko1922 · Piraeus (port of Athens)
    Rebetiko is the music of the Greek harbour districts, above all Piraeus near Athens.
  • Chanson1930 · Paris
    French chanson is song in which the lyrics take centre stage, sung with great expression, often with a small orchestra or just a piano or accordion.
  • Schlager1950 · Germany and the Netherlands
    Schlager is the German word for a hit, and it became the name for melodic, sentimental popular songs in German and Dutch.
  • Levenslied and smartlap1950 · Amsterdam (Jordaan)
    The levenslied, literally 'song of life', is the Dutch-language song about love, sorrow, mother and the city, with a simple melody that everyone can sing along to.
  • Brill Building and girl groups1958 · New York (Broadway)
    In the late 1950s, young songwriting duos such as Carole King and Gerry Goffin worked in small rooms with a piano in and around the Brill Building on Broadway, writing about what was on teenagers' minds.
  • Indopop1960 · The Netherlands
    Alongside the fast indorock, Indo-Dutch musicians in the Netherlands also made soft, melodic pop, with two-part vocal harmonies in the style of the Everly Brothers.
  • Kleinkunst1965 · The Netherlands
    Kleinkunst ('small art') is the Dutch-language song from cabaret and theatre, with the lyrics at the centre and a small accompaniment; in the 1950s, Wim Sonneveld, Toon Hermans and Jules de Corte were the big names.
  • Singer-songwriters1967 · Los Angeles
    Singer-songwriters wrote their own songs, sang them themselves and told of their own lives in the first person.
  • Swedish pop (ABBA)1974 · Stockholm
    ABBA won the Eurovision Song Contest in April 1974 with Waterloo.
  • New Romantic1979 · London
    In the late 1970s, you only got into the London club Blitz if you looked striking.
  • Neue Deutsche Welle1979 · West Germany
    Around 1979, a wave of German-language rock and pop grew out of punk and new wave in West Germany, which journalist Alfred Hilsberg called Neue Deutsche Welle ('New German Wave').
  • Nederpop1981 · The Netherlands
    Nederpop is the umbrella term for Dutch pop music.
  • British Eurobeat (Stock Aitken Waterman)1984 · London
    In the mid-1980s, a new pop sound emerged in England out of Italo disco and Hi-NRG: British Eurobeat.
  • Celtic new age1987 · Dublin
    Enya was a member of Clannad from 1980 to 1982 and then went solo, with producer Nicky Ryan and lyricist Roma Ryan.
  • Boy bands and girl groups1990 · London
    Every decade has its teen idol, with a recognisable face, a simple hook and a production that suits the medium; the 1990s brought the cast boy band and girl group.
  • Dutch-language song1994 · The Netherlands
    In the 1990s, the Dutch-language song grew to reach a large audience: from 1994 onwards, Marco Borsato had hit after hit with ballads.
  • Stockholm pop (Max Martin)1995 · Stockholm
    At the Cheiron Studio in Stockholm, Max Martin and his colleagues wrote and produced the hits of the Backstreet Boys, *NSYNC and Britney Spears from the mid-1990s onwards.
  • Streaming pop2018 · Atlanta and the internet
    Streaming pop is pop made for the way people have listened since the 2010s: through Spotify, YouTube and TikTok.
  • Hyperpop2019 · London and the internet
    Hyperpop exaggerates everything about pop: high, distorted voices with Auto-Tune, garish synthesisers, hard bass and sudden twists.

R&B, soul & funk

  • Jump blues1942 · New York
    Jump blues is the swing of a small band with horns: fast shuffles, boogie-woogie on the piano and a singer who entertains the audience.
  • Rhythm and blues1947 · Los Angeles
    After the Second World War big bands were too expensive to take on tour.
  • Doo-wop1950 · New York
    Doo-wop is harmony singing from the rhythm and blues of the fifties.
  • Soul1955 · Southern US
    Soul emerged in the mid-1950s from gospel, jazz and rhythm and blues.
  • Stax and Memphis soul1957 · Memphis
    In Memphis, Jim Stewart started the label Satellite in 1957; his sister Estelle Axton joined a year later, and from 1961 it was called Stax (STewart + AXton).
  • Motown1959 · Detroit
    In 1959, Berry Gordy started a label in Detroit that he organised like a car factory, with staff songwriters, the house band The Funk Brothers and a weekly quality control meeting.
  • Blue-eyed soul1964 · Los Angeles
    Blue-eyed soul is soul sung by white singers.
  • Funk1965 · United States
    Funk grew out of jazz, rhythm and blues and soul in the second half of the 1960s, with James Brown in the leading role.
  • Philly soul1968 · Philadelphia
    In Philadelphia, the songwriters and producers Kenneth Gamble and Leon Huff began making their own kind of soul in the late 1960s, and in 1971 they founded Philadelphia International Records.
  • Disco1970 · New York
    Disco emerged around 1970 in New York clubs for Black, Latino and gay audiences, such as The Loft and later the Paradise Garage, where the DJ blended records seamlessly into one another.
  • Northern soul1970 · Wigan
    In clubs in the north of England, such as the Twisted Wheel in Manchester and Wigan Casino (1973-1981), young people danced all night to fast American soul from the 1960s, often obscure singles that had sold poorly in America.
  • Go-go1975 · Washington D.C.
    Go-go is the funk of Washington D.C., a music that never stops.
  • Quiet storm1976 · Washington D.C.
    Quiet storm is soft, sensual soul for late at night.
  • Contemporary R&B1980 · United States
    The term rhythm and blues returned as R&B: vocals, production and drum machines take centre stage, and the producer becomes a star.
  • New jack swing1987 · New York
    In the late 1980s, in New York, producer Teddy Riley combined the vocals of R&B with the beats of hip hop: new jack swing.
  • Neo-soul1995 · United States
    Neo-soul is R&B that reached back to the soul of the 1970s, with Erykah Badu and D'Angelo among others.

Hip hop

  • Early hip hop1973 · The Bronx, New York
    On 11 August 1973, Kool Herc, who knew the sound systems of Kingston, played records at a party on Sedgwick Avenue in the Bronx.
  • Golden age hip hop1987 · New York
    In the late 1980s, samplers became affordable and producers built beats from fragments of existing recordings: the golden age of hip hop.
  • Gangsta rap1988 · Compton (Los Angeles)
    Gangsta rap took shape on the West Coast.
  • Nederhop1990 · Amsterdam
    From 1990, Osdorp Posse from Amsterdam rapped in broad Amsterdam dialect: hardcore rap about the street and the city.
  • Southern hip hop and crunk1990 · Atlanta, Houston and Memphis
    From the late 1980s, the South of the United States also developed its own hip hop: Geto Boys in Houston, Three 6 Mafia in Memphis and OutKast in Atlanta, who made their debut in 1994.
  • Boom bap1990 · New York
    Boom bap is the classic hip-hop of New York in the nineties.
  • G-funk1992 · Los Angeles
    Dr.
  • Trap2000 · Atlanta
    From the early 2000s, trap grew up in Atlanta: a hip hop style from the South of the United States, named after the trap house where drugs were sold.
  • Grime2003 · East London
    In the early 2000s, grime emerged in East London: rap in a London accent over fast, stark electronic beats of around 140 bpm, with influences from UK garage, jungle and dancehall.
  • Cloud rap2010 · New York
    Cloud rap sounds like hip-hop under water.
  • Drill2012 · Chicago and London
    Drill emerged around 2011 in Chicago: hard, dark rap about the violence in the neighbourhood, with Chief Keef as its best-known name.
  • Lo-fi hip hop2013 · Lyon (Lofi Girl)
    Lo-fi hip hop is calm, instrumental hip hop to study or relax to.

Electronic & dance

  • Moog and the first synthesizer pop1964 · Trumansburg (New York)
    From 1964, in Trumansburg, Robert Moog built synthesizers from separate modules that you connected with cables.
  • Kraftwerk and the man-machine1974 · Düsseldorf
    Kraftwerk was founded in 1970 in Düsseldorf and set aside guitar and drum kit for synthesizers, electronic drums and the vocoder.
  • Synth disco and Hi-NRG1977 · Munich
    In Munich in 1977, producer Giorgio Moroder made I Feel Love with Donna Summer, without a band: the bass line comes from a Moog driven by a sequencer.
  • Synthpop1978 · Sheffield
    Synthpop is the poppiest branch of new wave: Kraftwerk was the model for Gary Numan, OMD and The Human League, and later for the Pet Shop Boys.
  • Italo disco1980 · Italy
    Italy developed its own electronic dance music, a blend of synthpop and Eurodisco, with Giorgio Moroder as its great forerunner.
  • Garage1980 · New York
    The Paradise Garage in New York, with DJ Larry Levan, was one of the most important clubs in dance music from 1977 to 1987.
  • Detroit techno1981 · Detroit
    In Belleville, near Detroit, Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson grew up with the radio show of The Electrifying Mojo, who played Parliament alongside Kraftwerk.
  • Electro1982 · New York
    Afrika Bambaataa also played Kraftwerk at his parties in the Bronx.
  • House1984 · Chicago
    From 1977 Frankie Knuckles played a mix of disco, soul and European synthpop at The Warehouse in Chicago, for a crowd that was mostly Black and gay.
  • Acid house1987 · Chicago
    On Acid Tracks (1987) the Chicago group Phuture turned the knobs of a Roland TB-303, a flopped bass-line machine, while it was playing.
  • Rave1987 · England
    In 1987 English DJs came back from Ibiza and started clubs playing acid house; the summers of 1988 and 1989 became the Second Summer of Love.
  • Trance1990 · Frankfurt and Berlin
    Trance emerged around 1990 in Germany, mainly in Frankfurt and Berlin: electronic dance music with long build-ups, floating synthesiser chords and melodies that keep coming back.
  • Eurodance1991 · Belgium
    In the early nineties the European mainland danced to Eurodance: a solid house beat, a rapped verse and a sung chorus.
  • Trip hop1991 · Bristol
    Trip hop is a form of downtempo from the Bristol producer scene, where sound systems mixed reggae, hip hop, soul and punk.
  • Berlin techno1991 · Berlin
    After the fall of the Wall in 1989, many buildings in East Berlin stood empty.
  • Gabber and happy hardcore1992 · Rotterdam
    In the early nineties gabber emerged in Rotterdam: hardcore at 160 to more than 200 bpm, with a distorted bass drum that sounds like a hammer.
  • Jungle and drum and bass1992 · London
    In the early 1990s London producers sped up the breakbeats of rave to 160 to 180 bpm and set a deep, slow bassline beneath them, borrowed from reggae and dub: jungle.
  • IDM1992 · Sheffield
    IDM, intelligent dance music, is electronic music for listening to rather than dancing to.
  • Goa trance and psytrance1992 · Goa
    Goa trance emerged in the early nineties on the beaches of Goa in India, where travellers from Europe and Israel partied.
  • Downtempo, lounge and chill-out1993 · Ibiza
    Downtempo is slow, atmospheric electronic music with a beat.
  • Big beat1995 · Brighton
    Big beat combined the breakbeats of hip hop with rock guitars, samples and the sound of rave.
  • French house1997 · Paris
    From the mid-nineties producers in France such as Daft Punk, Cassius and Étienne de Crécy made French house: house tracks with filters and samples from old disco and funk.
  • UK garage1997 · London
    In the late 1990s, London DJs and producers turned American garage and house into a faster style of their own, with influences from jungle: UK garage, and later 2-step, with a skipping rhythm in which the kick drum does not fall on every beat.
  • Dutch trance2000 · The Netherlands
    Around 2000 trance became a Dutch export product.
  • Hardstyle2000 · Zaandam (Qlubtempo)
    Around 2000, hardstyle emerged in the Netherlands, out of German hard trance and Dutch hardcore.
  • Electroclash2001 · New York (Williamsburg, club Luxx)
    Around 2001, electroclash was a collision between electronic dance music and punk.
  • Dubstep2002 · London (Croydon)
    Dubstep emerged in the early 2000s in South London, around Croydon, out of UK garage, jungle and dub: a tempo of around 140 bpm, a rhythm with the snare on the third beat, so that it feels half as fast, and a heavy sub-bass.
  • Nu-disco2005 · Oslo
    Nu-disco brings back the dance floor of the seventies and eighties, but with modern machines.
  • Synthwave2006 · Paris
    Synthwave is electronic music that recreates the sound of the 1980s: analogue synthesizers, reverb-drenched drums, arpeggios borrowed from Italo disco, and images of neon and old films.
  • Dutch house2008 · Amsterdam
    Around 2008, Dutch DJs such as Chuckie, Afrojack, Laidback Luke and Hardwell made their own kind of house, also called dirty Dutch, after Chuckie's parties.
  • Moombahton2009 · Washington D.C.
    In late 2009, DJ Dave Nada was playing at a party thrown by his cousin in Washington.
  • EDM2010 · United States
    Around 2010 electronic dance music became the big new genre in the United States, under the name EDM.
  • Vaporwave2011 · Portland (Oregon)
    Vaporwave emerged on the internet around 2010.

Caribbean & Latin

  • Habanera1850 · Havana
    The habanera is a nineteenth-century Cuban dance, named after Havana.
  • Tango1890 · Buenos Aires
    Tango originated in the late nineteenth century in the port districts of Buenos Aires and Montevideo, where European immigrants, Afro-Argentines and people from the countryside came together.
  • Samba1917 · Rio de Janeiro
    Samba was born in the Afro-Brazilian neighbourhoods of Rio de Janeiro; Pelo Telefone (1917) is regarded as the first recorded samba.
  • Son1920 · Santiago de Cuba and Havana
    Son comes from the east of Cuba and became popular in Havana in the twenties.
  • Merengue1930 · Santo Domingo
    Merengue is the national dance of the Dominican Republic: fast music in 2/4 with accordion, the tambora, a two-headed drum, and the güira, a metal scraper.
  • Calypso1930 · Trinidad
    Calypso comes from Trinidad, where singers at carnival used humour and mockery to comment on politics and everyday life.
  • Mambo1938 · Havana and Mexico City
    Mambo grew out of danzón and son in Havana in the late thirties.
  • Mento1950 · Jamaica
    Mento is the folk music of rural Jamaica, played on banjo, guitar and the rumba box, a large thumb piano that provides the bass.
  • Cumbia1950 · Colombia (Caribbean coast)
    Cumbia comes from the Caribbean coast of Colombia, where African drums, Indigenous flutes and Spanish song forms came together.
  • Cha-cha-chá1953 · Havana
    The cha-cha-chá is a slower offshoot of the mambo that is easier to dance to, devised by violinist Enrique Jorrín of the Orquesta América in Havana.
  • Nueva canción1960 · Santiago de Chile
    Nueva canción, the 'new song', emerged in the early sixties in Chile and Argentina as an alliance of folk music, poetry and protest.
  • Tropicália and MPB1967 · Salvador
    In 1967 and 1968, Tropicália was the revolt of young Brazilian musicians against their own country's clichés.
  • Ska, rocksteady and reggae1968 · Kingston
    In Kingston, sound systems played American rhythm and blues; when those records became scarce, Jamaican producers started recording their own.
  • Salsa1970 · New York
    Salsa is the name that emerged in New York in the late 1960s for the Afro-Cuban dance music of Puerto Rican and Cuban musicians.
  • Dub1972 · Kingston
    In Kingston, producers made an instrumental version without vocals for the B-side of their singles.
  • 2 Tone1979 · Coventry
    Around 1969 the first skinheads adopted the ska and reggae of Jamaican migrants.
  • Dancehall1980 · Kingston
    Dancehall emerged in Jamaica in the 1980s, named after the dance venues where sound systems played.
  • Baile funk1989 · Rio de Janeiro (favelas)
    Baile funk, known in Brazil as funk carioca, emerged in the late 1980s at the dance parties (bailes) in the favelas of Rio de Janeiro.
  • Reggaeton1995 · San Juan (Puerto Rico)
    Reggaeton grew up in the 1990s in Panama and Puerto Rico out of Jamaican dancehall and hip hop, sung in Spanish.
  • Latin trap2016 · San Juan (Puerto Rico)
    Latin trap is trap in Spanish, from Puerto Rico.

Africa & Asia

  • Indian classical music1700 · North India
    The classical music of North India is built on the raga, a scale with fixed melodic turns and a mood of its own, and the tala, a rhythmic cycle.
  • Krontjong1920 · Batavia (Jakarta)
    Krontjong originated in Batavia, present-day Jakarta, from songs that Portuguese sailors brought with them from the sixteenth century onwards.
  • Highlife1950 · Accra
    Highlife grew up in Ghana in the first half of the twentieth century out of West African rhythms, church songs and the music of military bands and sailors.
  • Bollywood film music1950 · Mumbai
    In the Hindi films made in Mumbai, known as Bollywood, songs are a fixed part of the story.
  • Jùjú1950 · Lagos
    Jùjú is the popular music of the Yoruba in Nigeria.
  • Congolese rumba and soukous1955 · Kinshasa
    In the 1940s, Cuban records of son and rumba were popular in the Congo.
  • Mbaqanga1960 · Johannesburg
    Mbaqanga, the music of the township, emerged around 1960 in Johannesburg and Soweto.
  • Afrobeat1970 · Lagos
    Around 1970, in Lagos, Fela Kuti mixed highlife with jazz and the funk of James Brown: afrobeat.
  • Dangdut1970 · Jakarta
    Dangdut is the popular dance music of Indonesia.
  • Mandopop and Cantopop1970 · Hong Kong
    Mandopop and Cantopop are pop music in Mandarin and Cantonese.
  • Mbalax1975 · Dakar
    Mbalax is the dance music of Senegal.
  • City pop1978 · Tokyo
    City pop is the smooth, sunny Japanese pop of the late 1970s and the 1980s, made for a country that was growing rich: influences from American soft rock, funk and disco, played by tight session musicians.
  • Desert blues1979 · Kidal
    Desert blues, also called assouf, is the music of the Tuareg of the Sahara in Mali and Niger.
  • Raï1980 · Oran
    Raï comes from Oran in Algeria, where singers in cafés and at celebrations sang folk songs about love, drink and life, subjects that had no place in traditional music.
  • Bhangra1980 · Birmingham and Punjab
    Bhangra began as a harvest dance from the Punjab, driven by the deep beats of the dhol, a large drum.
  • J-pop1990 · Tokyo
    In the late 1980s the term J-pop came into use in Japan for modern Japanese pop music, distinct from the older, schlager-like kayōkyoku.
  • K-pop1992 · Seoul
    K-pop is South Korean pop music that grew from 1992 onwards, when Seo Taiji and Boys sang hip hop and rock in Korean.
  • Kwaito1992 · Johannesburg
    Kwaito is the music of South Africa's first free generation.
  • Afrobeats2010 · Lagos, Accra and London
    Afrobeats is the West African pop music of the 2010s: danceable tracks with a relaxed rhythm, vocals in English, Pidgin and Yoruba, and influences from dancehall, R&B and hip hop.
  • Gqom2010 · Durban
    Gqom is the dark, hard dance music of Durban.
  • Amapiano2015 · Pretoria and Johannesburg
    Amapiano emerged in the mid-2010s in the townships around Pretoria and Johannesburg, out of South African house and jazz.

Avant-garde & studio

  • Electronic studio and musique concrète1948 · Cologne
    In 1948 Pierre Schaeffer in Paris made music out of recorded sounds: musique concrète.
  • Cage and Fluxus1952 · New York
    John Cage wondered what music still is when the composer steps back.
  • Radiophonic Workshop1958 · London (Maida Vale)
    From 1958 the BBC had its own department for electronic sound, the Radiophonic Workshop, which made sounds and music for radio and television.
  • Minimal music1964 · New York and San Francisco
    In the 1960s, American composers made music out of short patterns that are repeated endlessly and change slowly.
  • Synthesizer orchestra, ambient and new age1976 · Paris
    Jean-Michel Jarre, who had worked with Pierre Schaeffer, recorded Oxygène in 1976 in a home studio in Paris.
  • Industrial1977 · London, Sheffield and Berlin
    In the late 1970s, Throbbing Gristle from London made music out of noise, tape loops and electronics, released on their own label, Industrial Records: industrial.
  • Noise1979 · Tokyo
    Noise makes music out of sheer racket: no scale, no metre, no chorus.

Technology

  • Saxophone1846 · Paris
    The Belgian instrument maker Adolphe Sax, born in Dinant, moved to Paris in 1842.
  • Phonograph1877 · Menlo Park, New Jersey
    In 1877, in his laboratory at Menlo Park, Thomas Edison built a machine that could capture sound and play it back.
  • Gramophone and 78 rpm record1887 · Washington D.C.
    Emile Berliner captured sound not on a cylinder but on a flat disc.
  • Steel guitar: lap steel and pedal steel1889 · Honolulu
    According to tradition, the Hawaiian schoolboy Joseph Kekuku discovered around 1889 in Honolulu that he could play a guitar by sliding a piece of metal along the strings.
  • Tin Pan Alley sheet music1890 · West 28th Street, New York
    Before the gramophone became commonplace, you bought a song on paper.
  • Pianola and piano roll1898 · Detroit
    In 1895, in Detroit, Edwin Votey built a device that made a piano play by itself.
  • Drum kit with foot pedal1909 · Chicago
    Around 1900 an orchestra often had several percussionists: one for the bass drum, one for the snare drum and the cymbals.
  • Radio1920 · Pittsburgh
    In November 1920 the station KDKA in Pittsburgh began regular broadcasts; in The Hague, Hanso Idzerda had already been broadcasting music in 1919.
  • Theremin1920 · Petrograd (Saint Petersburg)
    Around 1920 the Russian physicist Lev Termen built an instrument that you play without touching it.
  • Electrical recording and the microphone1925 · New York
    Until 1925 musicians sang into a large horn, which scratched the vibrations directly into the record.
  • Jukebox1927 · Grand Rapids, Michigan
    In 1927 AMI put a coin-operated machine on the market that was electrically amplified and could choose from ten records.
  • Magnetic tape1935 · Berlin
    At the radio exhibition in Berlin in 1935, AEG showed the Magnetophon, a recorder that captured sound on a tape with a magnetic coating made by BASF.
  • Hammond organ and Leslie1935 · Chicago
    In 1935, in Chicago, Laurens Hammond built an electric organ for churches that could not afford a pipe organ.
  • Electric guitar1936 · Kalamazoo, Michigan
    In a big band an acoustic guitar disappeared behind the horns.
  • Vocoder1938 · New York (Bell Labs)
    In the 1930s, at Bell Labs, Homer Dudley developed the vocoder, intended to send speech efficiently over telephone lines.
  • Steelpan1939 · Port of Spain, Trinidad
    In Trinidad, African drums were banned from carnival in the 1880s.
  • Long-playing record (LP)1948 · New York
    In 1948 Columbia introduced the long-playing record: a vinyl disc with finer grooves that turns at 33⅓ revolutions per minute.
  • 45 rpm single1949 · Camden, New Jersey
    In 1949 RCA Victor answered the LP with a small vinyl record with a large hole, turning at 45 revolutions per minute.
  • Fender Telecaster1950 · Fullerton, California
    The hollow electric guitar started to feed back when the amplifier was turned up loud.
  • Sound system1950 · Kingston
    From the fifties onwards, sound systems played in the poor neighbourhoods of Kingston: mobile discos with home-built amplifiers and enormous speaker cabinets.
  • Fender Precision Bass1951 · Fullerton, California
    A double bass is big, quiet and awkward to carry around.
  • Gibson Les Paul1952 · Kalamazoo, Michigan
    In 1952 Gibson answered Fender with a solid body of its own, designed together with the guitarist and inventor Les Paul.
  • Slapback echo1954 · Memphis
    In his small Sun studio in Memphis, Sam Phillips used two tape recorders to create a short echo: the sound was recorded and played back again a fraction of a second later.
  • Fender Stratocaster1954 · Fullerton, California
    In 1954 the Stratocaster followed: a solid body with a rounded shape, three pickups and a tremolo arm that lets you bend the pitch down and spring it back.
  • Transistor radio1954 · Indianapolis
    The Regency TR-1 of 1954 was the first transistor radio to go on sale in the shops.
  • Plate reverb (EMT 140)1957 · Lahr, Germany
    Reverb is the sound that still hangs in a hall after a note has been played.
  • Multitrack recording1957 · Redwood City, California
    As early as around 1950, Les Paul was recording parts on top of one another by copying from one tape recorder to another.
  • Stereo1958 · New York
    As early as 1931 the British engineer Alan Blumlein applied for a patent on a way of recording sound on two channels, so that you can hear where an instrument is placed.
  • Rhythm machine1959 · Cincinnati, Ohio
    In 1959 the organ maker Wurlitzer released the Side Man, a cabinet with a rotating disc that triggered electronic drum sounds in the rhythm of a waltz, a rumba or a foxtrot.
  • Amplifier and overdrive1962 · London (Hanwell)
    An amplifier is supposed to make a signal bigger without changing it.
  • Fuzz pedal1962 · Nashville
    During a Marty Robbins recording session in Nashville, one channel of the mixing desk broke down.
  • Mellotron1963 · Birmingham
    Under every key of a Mellotron sits a strip of tape holding a recording of a real instrument: a violin, a flute, a choir.
  • Wall of Sound1963 · Los Angeles (Gold Star Studios)
    At Gold Star Studios in Los Angeles, producer Phil Spector had several guitarists, pianists and drummers play the same part at the same time, and sent the sound through an echo chamber.
  • Compact cassette1963 · Hasselt
    Lou Ottens's team at Philips in Hasselt developed a small plastic box containing magnetic tape, which was presented in 1963.
  • Moog synthesizer1964 · Trumansburg, New York
    From 1964, in Trumansburg, Robert Moog built synthesizers from separate modules that you connected with cables.
  • 8-track1965 · Detroit, Michigan
    In 1964 aircraft maker Bill Lear designed the Stereo 8: a cartridge with an endless loop of magnetic tape and eight tracks, four stereo programmes side by side.
  • Fender Rhodes1965 · Fullerton, California
    Harold Rhodes developed an electric piano without strings.
  • The studio as instrument1966 · London (Abbey Road)
    In 1966 the Beatles stopped touring and began working mainly in the studio.
  • Wah-wah pedal1966 · Sepulveda, Los Angeles
    In 1966 Brad Plunkett at the Thomas Organ Company developed a pedal with a filter that you open and close with your foot.
  • Minimoog1970 · Trumansburg, New York
    The big modular Moog was a cabinet full of cables, made for a studio.
  • Technics SL-1200 and scratching1972 · Osaka (Kadoma)
    In 1972 Matsushita released the SL-1200 under the Technics name, a direct-drive turntable.
  • Talk box1973 · Marissa, Illinois
    A talkbox makes a guitar talk.
  • The mixing desk as instrument (dub)1973 · Kingston
    In Kingston, producers made an instrumental version without vocals for the B-side of their singles.
  • Sequential Prophet-51978 · San Jose, California
    The Minimoog could only play one note at a time.
  • Fairlight CMI1979 · Sydney
    In 1979 the Australians Peter Vogel and Kim Ryrie released the Fairlight CMI, a computer with a keyboard and a light pen.
  • Sony Walkman1979 · Tokyo
    Sony co-founder Masaru Ibuka wanted to listen to music on long flights.
  • TASCAM Portastudio1979 · Musashino, Tokyo
    In the seventies a multitrack studio was expensive and large.
  • Linn LM-11980 · Los Angeles
    In 1980 Roger Linn released the LM-1, the first drum machine to use digital recordings of real drums instead of electronically imitated sounds.
  • Gated reverb1980 · Townhouse Studios, London
    Gated reverb means reverb with a gate.
  • Roland TR-8081980 · Osaka
    The Roland TR-808 came onto the market in 1980 as an aid for making demos.
  • Roland TB-3031981 · Osaka
    The TB-303 Bass Line was meant for guitarists who practised alone and missed having a bass player.
  • MTV and the music video1981 · New York
    On 1 August 1981 MTV launched in the United States, a television channel that broadcast music videos all day long.
  • Compact disc (CD)1982 · Eindhoven
    Philips and Sony jointly developed the compact disc, which came onto the market in 1982.
  • Yamaha DX71983 · Hamamatsu
    The Yamaha DX7 of 1983 did not work with filters, but with FM synthesis: one wave makes the frequency of another go up and down very rapidly.
  • MIDI1983 · Anaheim, California

  • Roland TR-9091983 · Osaka
    The TR-909 of 1983 was the successor to the 808.
  • Beat sampler (SP-1200, MPC)1987 · Scotts Valley, California

  • Pro Tools and the DAW1991 · Menlo Park, California
    In 1991 Digidesign released Pro Tools, a system that let you record and edit multiple tracks on an Apple computer without tape.
  • Breakbeat sampling (the Amen break)1992 · London

  • Auto-Tune1997 · Los Gatos, California

  • FL Studio (FruityLoops)1998 · Ghent

  • MP3 and Napster1999 · Boston

  • Ableton Live2001 · Berlin

  • iPod and the iTunes Store2001 · Cupertino, California

  • YouTube2005 · San Mateo, California
    YouTube started in 2005 as a website where anyone could share videos.
  • SoundCloud2007 · Berlin
    SoundCloud was founded in 2007 by the Swedes Alexander Ljung and Eric Wahlforss and made its home in Berlin.
  • Streaming (Spotify)2008 · Stockholm
    In October 2008 Spotify launched in Stockholm.
  • TikTok2018 · Beijing
    TikTok, the short-video app from the Chinese company ByteDance, broke through worldwide in 2018.
